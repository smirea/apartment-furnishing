MDF with the profile routed into the face and about five coats of primer sanded
flat, so paint goes straight on with no prep. A PVC film on the back keeps the
panel rigid. Three profiles share one panel width, one box size and one install

guide covers what an installer or specifier needs to measure a wall, price the
job, put it up and paint it.

The three profiles compared
One build, one panel width, one box. The differences that matter on site are the cut depth, the
panel thickness, what happens at a join, and the fact that Ridge stops at 106″.

P R O FILE S · D R AW N T O S C A LE

What the cut leaves
Each profile is routed into the face of the board. All three stop at the same place: 0.06″ of
board under the deepest point of the cut. That is what is left holding the panel together, and
it is why the deeper profiles are the thicker panels rather than the thinner ones.

Start at a corner
Corners are what people look at, so start at one and work away from it,
letting the cut panel land at the far end — ideally the end that dies into
another corner, a cabinet or trim.
A cut edge has no profile running off it, so on Ridge and Valley it shows a
plain flat edge rather than a half trough or scoop. Plan for that edge to finish
into something rather than meet another panel.

  1      Prep the wall
         Start with a clean, dry, flat wall. Take off outlet covers and switch plates and pull any nails or hooks. The panel
         follows the wall rather than straightening it, so fill or sand anything that would telegraph through — and on a
         profile this shallow, a bump shows. Adhesive needs a sound surface: scrape loose paint and let fresh plaster
         cure.

  2      Mark your layout
         Start at the corner you chose on page 4. Draw a plumb line on the first panel’s leading edge — corners are rarely
         plumb, so trust the line, not the wall. Everything else follows it, so check it twice.

  3      Which way round
         Ridge and Valley are symmetrical — both panel edges are cut at the lowest point, so either end can lead and a
         panel can be turned end for end without anything showing.
         Slat is not. One edge is the top outer corner of a slat and the other is the groove floor, so the two edges are not
         the same. Keep every Slat panel the same way round as you work across the wall. Turn one and you will either
         butt two slat faces together with no channel between them, or open a channel twice the width of the rest — and
         on a painted wall both read instantly.

  4      No battens
         These go straight onto drywall, plaster or timber. There is nothing to build out first, and no cavity to leave — the
         panel is fixed flat to the wall with adhesive and held while it grabs. That keeps the finished face at 0.20″ to 0.37″
         off the wall depending on profile, so skirting, architrave and outlet plates mostly still sit right.

  6      Cut to fit
         Primed MDF cuts with a handsaw, jigsaw or circular saw. Cutting to length is a plain crosscut. Cutting to width is
         a choice, because where you rip decides what the new edge can do.
         Rip at a low point — the channel, the trough, the scoop bottom — and you reproduce the edge the factory puts
         on a panel, so the cut panel still butts cleanly against the next one. Only 0.06″ of board sits on that line, so back it
         up and use a fine blade. Rip at a high point for a stronger, full-thickness edge — but the profile will not line up
         against another panel, so send that edge into a corner. Either way the cut edge is raw MDF: prime it before
         painting.

          The panel arrives with about five coats of primer, sanded flat, so there is no sanding or priming to do on the
